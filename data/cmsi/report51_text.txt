Main Findings
• China’s merchant marine is a foundational economic and national security asset, supporting the
country’s multi-decade growth and modernization and military logistics. Thus, Beijing views
seafarers as a strategic resource and has prioritized development initiatives to strengthen the
quality and quantity of the nation’s professional merchant marine.
• China has a large potential labor supply of seafarers, with 943,954 registered for international
and coastal shipping in 2023, but the actual number of seafarers actively working is far fewer.
Over the past decade the sector has shifted from oversupply to emerging shortages due to
COVID-19 disruptions, declining interest in maritime careers, workforce aging, and other factors.
China will face continued challenges in sustaining its merchant marine workforce.
• Unique to China, some of its state-owned shipping fleets maintain a system of Ship Party
Branches and ship political commissars to ensure the Chinese Communist Party is represented in
the ship’s leadership structure. Official regulations and laws do not explicitly acknowledge the
presence of ship political commissars, but they nevertheless comprise an important part of the
decision-making body aboard.
• Merchant marine careers are an attractive transition path for veterans who can qualify for officer
licenses or rating positions through training and examinations. To assist with job placement and
boost veteran entry into the workforce, the PRC government launched “Project Wave Break” in
2020, pairing up maritime schools, shipping companies, and authorities to facilitate this
transition. These programs have expanded across China’s provinces over the past several years.
• While veterans are traditionally sought after for service in the PRC’s militia forces, the closer
integration between training programs and companies by “Project Wave Break” has facilitated a
pathway for veteran seafarers to join the maritime militia. Outside of major state-owned shipping
enterprises, however, the lack of stable lifelong careers with companies is not conducive to the
formation of militia organizations in enterprises.
• Despite improving regulation, information access, and standardization, China’s mariner
qualification and certification system faces persistent fraud and abuse, including falsified sea
time, forged certificates, exam cheating, and data manipulation, which create hidden safety risks.
There is significant fraud and abuse found in the many private crew service agencies that provide
manning and crew management services for shipping companies. The Maritime Safety
Administration conducts regular crackdowns, imposes fines, suspends licenses, and implements a
national demerit points system, but has proven unable to fully eradicate the problem.
• COSCO Shipping’s subsidiary COSCO Shipping Seafarer Management Co. Ltd. is the largest
crew manning provider in China and has a robust in-house seafarer management system. In
contrast, many companies outsource crew management to agencies that provide freelance
seafarer labor. This has contributed to labor instability and other concerns, prompting the
government to push firms to assume greater direct responsibility for their workforces.
• Despite major headwinds in the seafaring profession, the merchant marine remains a robust
component of China’s overall maritime power. Shipping and the merchant marine will continue
to be central to the PRC’s goal of becoming a “Transport Power” and the availability of
commercial assets for military use will gradually improve.
Introduction
The People’s Republic of China (PRC) is one of the world’s top seafarer suppliers, accounting for
about one-third of the global workforce.1 Its merchant marine has physically supported the PRC’s
rapid, multi-decade economic growth and continues to serve as a strategic workforce underpinning
the nation’s interaction with the global economy. This mammoth force enables the movement of key
strategic inputs, such as crude oil, minerals, and other raw material imports, as well as countless
other national outputs. Globally, seafarers are integral to supporting Beijing’s geostrategic goals, such
as regional shipping routes along the Maritime Silk Road.
The merchant marine is also critical for PRC national security and the People’s Liberation Army’s
(PLA) strategic mobility. Under the national strategy of Military-Civil Fusion, China has created
numerous ship transport units as part of the strategic delivery support forces (战略投送支援力量),
including units in product tanker, bulk cargo, Roll-on/Roll-off ferries, salvage, and other various
fleets of ships.2 This effort seeks to provide the PLA with sufficient transport capacity in times of
need, including both East Asian contingencies, such as an attack on Taiwan, and defense of China’s
“overseas interests.”3 China’s merchant marine has historically been and will continue to be a critical
manning resource in support of military seaborne logistics.4
Given the merchant marine’s vital economic and military value, the PRC regards it as “an important
strategic resource” that must be nurtured and developed.5 Beijing has prioritized this critical
workforce in many government documents and development initiatives.6 This report examines the

China’s Shipping Industry
China leads the world in many indicators of maritime shipping power. It boasts one of the largest
flagged merchant fleets at well over 12,000 vessels and 340 million dead weight tons (PRC and Hong
Kong SAR combined) in January 2024. This includes the world’s largest tanker fleet at 1,741 vessels
(not including those registered to Hong Kong SAR), and large fleets of bulk and container vessels.7
These figures do not include the large number of PRC-owned, foreign-flagged vessels operating
internationally. This could be well over 2,000 vessels.8 These figures also do not include the vast
fleets of inland waterway vessels, numbering around 98,700.9

Ship Organization
The organizational system aboard PRC merchant ships is similar to international practices, with the
addition of some Chinese Communist Party (CCP) elements. As with other nations’ vessels, there is a
deck department (舱面部 or 甲板部), engine department (轮机部 or 机舱部 ), and the steward
department (业务部 ).29 The deck department oversees navigation, cargo handling, and management;
the engine department is responsible for vessel propulsion, maintenance, and equipment; and the
steward department manages food, sanitation, and accommodations for the crew. Figure 2 (below)
situates each ship position and its equivalent term in English. The ship hierarchy is essential to
ensuring command and operation of any vessel, with ultimate authority belonging to the captain.30

The PRC’s merchant marine officers (高级船员) and ratings (普通船员) are largely aligned with
international norms in terms of specific ranks and duties. While officer ranks are fairly
straightforward, the ratings, or support-grade personnel, merit some explanation because they are not
intuitive to those unfamiliar with merchant marine careers. Among the ratings, there are entry-level
ordinary seaman (OS, 二级水手 ), who are starting their careers and perform various deck work and
cargo handling functions under the direction of the senior rating onboard. After gaining experience
and training, an OS may upgrade to become an able-bodied seaman (AB, 一级水手 ). From there, the
rank of a rating depends on their experience and qualification to participate in a navigation or engine
watch. Watch standing is a critical aspect of ship operation and must be maintained at all times.
While officers are qualified to organize and keep a watch as the Officer in Charge of the Navigational
Watch (OICNW, 驾驶员), they must work in shifts and are assisted by ratings that qualify to
participate in watch standing duties.36 With sufficient experience, training, and passing an exam, an
AB can become a “rating forming part of a navigational watch” (RFPNW, 值班水手 ) or a “rating
forming part of an engine watch” (RFPEW, 值班机工) . This qualification allows a rating to join a
navigational watch and perform watch standing duties such as maintaining a helm watch or standing
lookout. From there, a rating may advance to become an able seafarer – deck (AS-D, 高级值班水手)
or able seafarer – engine (AS-E, 高级值班机工). Lastly, the most senior rating aboard a ship is
referred to as the boatswain or bosun (水手长), who oversees the work of the ratings in the deck
department. There is an equivalent senior rating in the engine department, called the chief motorman
(机工长).37

seafarers with greater responsibilities. Officers begin as either third officer or fourth engineer and

Merchant mariner qualifications will be discussed in greater detail in later sections, but it is important
to note here the three navigational zones in which they may be licensed to work. Navigational zones
are divided into “unlimited navigation” (无限航区) , “coastal navigation” (沿海航区 ), and “inland
waterways” (内河). Unlimited navigation refers to any navigable waters at sea, including open ports
and international navigable canals and rivers in any country. Coastal navigation refers to ports, inland
waterways, territorial waters, and all other navigable waters under PRC jurisdiction.38 The different
zones act as restrictions on a mariner’s license. A mariner licensed only for inland waterways would
be restricted to just that zone and would need to qualify for zone expansion.
Lastly, merchant mariners are sometimes referred to by the type of labor contract under which they
work. Stated simply, traditional labor contracts between seafarers and shipping companies or
shipowners are referred to as “in-house seafarers” (自有船员) while others rely on a labor contract
established between seafarers and a crew service agency that provides manning services for shipping
companies, often referred to as “freelance seafarers” (自由船员). The latter category is more fluid in
the labor market as they typically do not see themselves as employees of the major shipping
companies, simply hired labor.39
Unique to Chinese merchant shipping, the CCP also maintains a system of control over China’s state-
owned oceangoing merchant fleets through Party branches and ship political commissars. Mention of
this system is absent from most relevant regulations and laws pertaining to China’s merchant marine.
Nonetheless, the ship Party branch serves as an important decision-making body and leadership
structure on board. It is composed of the “Three Chiefs” (三长), i.e., the three senior officers aboard
the vessel. The Party Branch is led by the Party secretary, a position held by the ship’s political
commissar. The ship captain serves as the deputy secretary and the chief engineer as a branch
member.40 These individuals likely provide their own perspectives in ship decision-making, as it
pertains to their professional roles, but it is unknown how big a role the Party branch plays in specific
planning of ship operations.

Labor Supply and Demand
The PRC is one of the world’s leading suppliers of seafarers, alongside countries like the Philippines,
Ukraine, India, and Russia, fulfilling both international and domestic maritime labor needs. By the
end of 2023, the PRC had just under two million “registered mariners” (注册船员 ). This large figure
is an aggregate counting of all merchant marine trades across various industries in China, including
crew registered to work in inland waterways, coastal routes, and international routes. It does not
include the 143,582 “dispatched seafarers” (外派海员 ) reported that year.44 This term refers to
Chinese seafarers hired to work aboard foreign-owned vessels, also including vessels registered to
Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan. The total number of registered mariners also does not include the
nearly 16 million people working in the fishing industry nationally in 2023.45 The total figures of
registered mariners has continued to gradually expand over the past decade. These large figures are
frequently noted publicly, but are not reflective of the actual active workforce, as will be explored
below.

The mid-to-late 2010s saw an oversupply in Chinese seafarers in unlimited navigational zones and
some shortages in coastal. The 2019 index report provided longitudinal data to highlight the growing
disparity between the number of registered seafarers and those that actually worked on board vessels.
From 2014-2018, the number of registered seafarers of all ranks in unlimited navigation areas grew
from 447,054 to 545,877, while those that recorded sea time onboard vessels only grew from 254,555
to 258,246. This means that 56.94 percent of seafarers worked aboard vessels in 2014, compared to
only 47.31 percent in 2018. The figures worsen to 50.26 percent and 42.01 percent respectively when
narrowing it down to those who recorded more than three months of sea time. According to the
report, this resulted from an oversupply of seafarers across all ranks, but particularly in ratings and
some officer positions. Conversely, supply and demand for coastal zone seafarers during this same
period experienced dramatic fluctuations and was considered to be in an overall shortage by 2018.54
These dual trends of severe oversupply in unlimited navigation zones and shortages in coastal
navigational zones would continue in 2019.55
The oversupply situation changed significantly in 2020, primarily due to the COVID-19 pandemic
and its negative impact on global shipping. Unlimited navigation seafarers experienced a clear shift
toward demand outpacing supply, with intensifying shortages in third mates, fourth engineers, and
some ratings. This was likely due to pandemic restrictions on international crew relief and large
numbers of unlimited navigation seafarers transferring to domestic coastal routes. Coastal zone
seafarer labor supply continued to experience both oversupply and shortages: that is, there were
severe shortages in some positions, such as third officer, fourth engineer, motorman, and captain,
with severe oversupply of second officers and second engineers. Overall, the index considered
coastal zone labor supply to be in general undersupply. Those actively working aboard coastal
navigation vessels grew to over 65 percent while unlimited navigation seafarers actively logging sea
time dropped to just 32.11 percent.56
According to the overall assessment of the 2021 CSSDI report, China is experiencing a general
reduction in the number of seafarers that could result in an overall seafarer shortage (全面性海员紧
缺).57 China is not alone in this situation as the global seafarer labor market has experienced critical
shortages. As one of the primary nations supplying global seafarers, these international and domestic
labor trends are interrelated and acutely felt in China.58
